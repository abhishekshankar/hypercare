## What dementia is, and is not

"Dementia" is not a single disease. It is an umbrella term that describes a pattern: memory and thinking changes that are big enough to interfere with daily life—for example, needing help with finances, getting lost in familiar neighborhoods, or struggling to manage medications independently [1]. Underneath that umbrella are several different diseases that produce the pattern in different ways. When a clinician says "dementia," they are describing what they observe; when they name a specific disease, they are telling you what they think is causing it. Both can be true at the same time. Sometimes the specific cause is not yet known, and that is honest medicine, not bad medicine.

Mild cognitive impairment, or MCI, is the step before dementia. It means measurable changes in thinking that are greater than expected for age, but not yet significant enough to disrupt daily life—for example, forgetting appointments or recent conversations more often than usual, repeating questions, or needing extra time to complete familiar tasks, while still being able to manage personal care and basic household activities independently [1]. Not everyone with MCI progresses to dementia. Some people remain stable for years. Some improve, especially if a treatable cause is found—such as medication side effects, sleep apnea, or depression [2].

When a spouse is being evaluated for dementia, the diagnosis lands differently than for any other relationship. This is the person you chose, the one who knows your history, who balances your strengths, who you imagined growing old with in a particular way. The words "Alzheimer's" or "frontotemporal dementia" do not just describe a medical condition—they rewrite the future you planned together. You may find yourself grieving the partnership while the person is still very much present, which can feel disloyal or premature. It is neither. It is the heart acknowledging a profound shift before the mind has fully processed it.

## The most common types, in plain language

**Alzheimer's disease** is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60–80% of cases [1]. It typically begins with difficulty remembering recent events or conversations. People may repeat questions, misplace items in unusual places, or struggle to find words. The progression is usually gradual, over several years, with memory, language, and orientation affected as the disease advances.

**Vascular dementia** results from impaired blood flow to the brain—often following strokes or due to small vessel disease [1]. Unlike Alzheimer's, it can appear suddenly or progress in noticeable steps that correspond to vascular events. Planning, attention, and processing speed are often affected early; significant memory loss may come later.

**Lewy body dementia** involves abnormal protein deposits (Lewy bodies) that also occur in Parkinson's disease [1]. Distinctive features include vivid visual hallucinations, fluctuating alertness from hour to hour, REM sleep behavior disorder (acting out dreams), and pronounced sensitivity to certain antipsychotic medications that can worsen symptoms severely [3]. This type matters to identify because standard treatments for agitation or psychosis in other dementias can be dangerous here.

**Frontotemporal dementia (FTD)** typically appears earlier than other dementias, often in the 50s and 60s [1]. Memory may remain intact initially while personality, judgment, social behavior, or language change dramatically. People who know the person well often notice these shifts before any formal testing confirms them.

**Mixed dementia** means more than one type is contributing simultaneously. This is common, especially in older adults. A diagnosis of "probable Alzheimer's disease with vascular contribution" is not a contradiction—it is a more accurate description of complex brain changes [1].

## How a diagnosis is actually made

The diagnostic pathway for Alzheimer's disease has changed significantly. As of 2025–2026, blood biomarker tests are now the first-line option, with brain scans and spinal taps reserved for confirmation or special cases [4].

**Blood biomarkers first.** Two blood tests are now FDA-cleared for clinical use:
- **pTau217** (Fujirebio Lumipulse assay, cleared May 2025) [5]
- **pTau181** (Roche Elecsys assay, cleared October 2025—the first Alzheimer's blood test specifically cleared for primary care use) [6]

These tests measure phosphorylated tau proteins, which rise when Alzheimer's-related brain changes are present. When compared to amyloid PET scans, blood pTau217 shows 91.7% positive agreement and 97.3% negative agreement—meaning a negative result is highly reliable for ruling out Alzheimer's pathology [4].

**The new pathway works like this:** Your partner's primary care clinician can now order a blood biomarker test as an initial step. If the result is clearly negative, Alzheimer's is unlikely. If positive, a specialist may confirm with additional testing or proceed with diagnosis depending on the clinical picture [4].

**What else is involved:** Blood biomarkers are powerful but not standalone. A complete workup still includes [7]:
- Ask about changes in memory, thinking, and daily functioning over the past 6–12 months [10]
- MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) or MMSE (Mini-Mental State Exam) score [11]
- Check reflexes, eye movements, gait, and look for signs of Parkinsonism or stroke [12]
- TSH, vitamin B12, metabolic panel, and infection screening [13]
- MRI to look for strokes, tumors, or brain atrophy patterns [14]

**Shared medical decision-making:** As spouses, you have likely made health decisions together for years. The diagnosis may shift this dynamic gradually or suddenly. Your partner may still have capacity to understand options and express preferences, or you may already be noticing gaps between what they say they want and what seems wise. Document your partner's wishes while they can still articulate them clearly: preferences about future living arrangements, treatments they would want or refuse, how they feel about clinical trials. These conversations are tender and can feel frightening, but they are an act of respect—preserving your partner's voice for the time when they may no longer be able to speak for themselves.

**Financial awareness:** If your partner has managed the household finances, investments, or bill-paying, this is the moment to create transparency together. You need to know where accounts are held, what bills are due when, and how to access funds if your partner becomes unable to manage them. This is not about taking over; it is about ensuring continuity. If your partner is resistant, frame it as protection: "If something happened to you, I wouldn't know how to keep things running. Can you show me?" Consider meeting with a financial advisor together to plan for the long-term costs of care.

## When the answer is "we are not sure"

Sometimes the workup ends without a clean label. You may hear phrases like "cognitive impairment, etiology unclear" or "dementia, type to be determined." This uncertainty can feel harder than a definitive diagnosis, but it is often the most honest position early in the disease course [7].

The pattern may not yet be specific enough to distinguish between types. The plan in these cases typically involves: addressing depression, sleep apnea, and blood pressure [19], tracking changes in memory and daily abilities [20], repeating the MoCA or MMSE in 6–12 months [21], and reassessing as the picture clarifies [7].

You are allowed to seek a second opinion if uncertainty is blocking necessary planning. A good clinician will not be offended by this request.

## What you can do next

Before your partner's next appointment, consider these questions. You will not get through all of them, and that is fine—prioritize what keeps you awake at night [7]:

- What specific dementia type do you think this is, and how sure are you? [22]
- Are we still looking for other causes like infections or vitamin deficiencies? [23]
- Should we treat depression, sleep apnea, or medication side effects? [24]
- How far along do we estimate the changes are? [25]
- What new symptoms like sudden confusion or falls should prompt an earlier call? [26]
- Are you suggesting any medications, and what should they help with—or not help with? [27]
- Would a research study for new Alzheimer's treatments be a fit? [28]
- When should we come back, and what improvements or changes should we expect? [29]

Write the answers down during the appointment. Memory is unreliable for everyone in high-stakes conversations, including you.

## How to care for yourself in this season

The diagnosis of a spouse creates a particular kind of grief—ambiguous loss, where the person is physically present but psychologically changing. You may mourn the companion you had while caring for the one you have now. You may feel lonely in your own home, or guilty for wanting connection your partner can no longer provide. You may notice small losses before others do: the way they no longer follow the plot of a movie you both loved, the conversations that circle back to the same questions, the plans that feel increasingly uncertain.

"In sickness and in health" takes on new weight now. It does not mean you must do everything alone, or that your own needs cease to matter. It means you are choosing to stay present to a changing reality, which requires its own kind of courage. Build support that sustains you: friends who can listen without trying to fix, a therapist who understands caregiver grief, activities that reconnect you with who you are beyond this role. Your wellbeing matters not as a luxury but as a necessity—you cannot pour from an empty cup, and this will be a long road. The love that brought you together can still be honored, even as its expression changes.
