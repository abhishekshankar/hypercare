## What dementia is, and is not

"Dementia" is not a single disease. It is an umbrella term that describes a pattern: memory and thinking changes that are big enough to interfere with daily life—for example, needing help with finances, getting lost in familiar neighborhoods, or struggling to manage medications independently [1]. Underneath that umbrella are several different diseases that produce the pattern in different ways. When a clinician says "dementia," they are describing what they observe; when they name a specific disease, they are telling you what they think is causing it. Both can be true at the same time. Sometimes the specific cause is not yet known, and that is honest medicine, not bad medicine.

Mild cognitive impairment, or MCI, is the step before dementia. It means measurable changes in thinking that are greater than expected for age, but not yet significant enough to disrupt daily life—for example, forgetting appointments or recent conversations more often than usual, repeating questions, or needing extra time to complete familiar tasks, while still being able to manage personal care and basic household activities independently [1]. Not everyone with MCI progresses to dementia. Some people remain stable for years. Some improve, especially if a treatable cause is found—such as medication side effects, sleep apnea, or depression [2].

## The most common types, in plain language

**Alzheimer's disease** is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60–80% of cases [1]. It is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau tangles in the brain, leading to progressive neurodegeneration. It typically begins with difficulty remembering recent events or conversations. People may repeat questions, misplace items in unusual places, or struggle to find words. The progression is usually gradual, over several years, with memory, language, and orientation affected as the disease advances.

**Vascular dementia** results from impaired blood flow to the brain—often following strokes or due to small vessel disease [1]. Unlike Alzheimer's, it can appear suddenly or progress in noticeable steps that correspond to vascular events such as strokes, transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), or progressive small vessel damage. Planning, attention, and processing speed are often affected early; significant memory loss may come later.

**Lewy body dementia** involves abnormal protein deposits (Lewy bodies) that also occur in Parkinson's disease [1]. Distinctive features include vivid visual hallucinations (such as seeing people, animals, or objects that aren't there), fluctuating alertness from hour to hour (where the person may seem perfectly clear one moment and confused or drowsy the next), REM sleep behavior disorder (acting out dreams, which can involve punching, kicking, or shouting during sleep), and pronounced sensitivity to certain antipsychotic medications (like haloperidol or thioridazine) that can worsen symptoms severely [3]. This type matters to identify because standard treatments for agitation or psychosis in other dementias (such as typical antipsychotics) can be dangerous here.

**Frontotemporal dementia (FTD)** typically appears earlier than other dementias, often in the 50s and 60s [1]. Memory may remain intact initially while personality, judgment, social behavior, or language change dramatically. For example, a person might make impulsive financial decisions, say inappropriate things in social settings, lose empathy for family members, or struggle to form coherent sentences. People who know the person well often notice these shifts before any formal testing confirms them.

**Mixed dementia** means more than one type is contributing simultaneously. This is common, especially in older adults. A diagnosis of "probable Alzheimer's disease with vascular contribution" is not a contradiction—it is a more accurate description of complex brain changes [1].

## How a diagnosis is actually made

The diagnostic pathway for Alzheimer's disease has changed significantly. As of 2025–2026, blood biomarker tests are now the first-line option, with brain scans and spinal taps reserved for confirmation or special cases [4].

**Blood biomarkers first.** Two blood tests are now FDA-cleared for clinical use:
- **pTau217** (Fujirebio Lumipulse assay, cleared May 2025) measures phosphorylated tau at threonine 217, which increases when Alzheimer's-related neurofibrillary tangle pathology is present [5]
- **pTau181** (Roche Elecsys assay, cleared October 2025—the first Alzheimer's blood test specifically cleared for primary care use) measures phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 [6]

These tests detect phosphorylated tau fragments in blood that correlate with brain tau pathology. When compared to amyloid PET scans, blood pTau217 shows 91.7% positive agreement and 97.3% negative agreement—meaning a negative result is highly reliable for ruling out Alzheimer's pathology [4].

**The new pathway works like this:** Your primary care clinician can now order a blood biomarker test as an initial step. If the result is clearly negative (below established cutoffs), Alzheimer's is unlikely. If positive or indeterminate, a specialist may confirm with additional testing or proceed with diagnosis depending on the overall clinical picture [4]. This shifts the traditional model where patients waited months for specialist appointments and expensive PET scans.

**What else is involved:** Blood biomarkers are powerful but not standalone. A complete diagnostic workup still includes [7]:
- Ask about changes in memory, thinking, and daily functioning over the past 6–12 months [10]
- MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) or MMSE (Mini-Mental State Exam) score [11]
- Check reflexes, eye movements, gait, and look for signs of Parkinsonism or stroke [12]
- TSH, vitamin B12, metabolic panel, and infection screening [13]
- MRI to look for strokes, tumors, or brain atrophy patterns [14]

**When PET scans or spinal taps are still used:** Amyloid PET scans and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests remain valuable for [4][7]:
- Confirming Alzheimer's when blood test results are unclear or don't match symptoms [15]
- Rapidly progressive symptoms needing tests for Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease [16]
- Research studies or clinical trials needing biomarker proof [17]
- Patients under 65 with unusual symptoms needing precise diagnosis [18]

**Disease-modifying therapies exist for early Alzheimer's.** Lecanemab and donanemab are FDA-approved for early-stage Alzheimer's disease based on amyloid clearance and clinical slowing of decline [8][9]. These medications can slow decline in eligible patients with confirmed amyloid pathology. Determining eligibility, understanding risks (including amyloid-related imaging abnormalities or ARIA), and making treatment decisions require detailed discussion with specialists—this module defers to the medical-dmt module for that guidance.


## When the answer is "we are not sure"

Sometimes the workup ends without a clean label. You may hear phrases like "cognitive impairment, etiology unclear" or "dementia, type to be determined." This uncertainty can feel harder than a definitive diagnosis, but it is often the most honest position early in the disease course [7].

The pattern may not yet be specific enough to distinguish between etiologies. The plan in these cases typically involves: addressing depression, sleep apnea, and blood pressure [19], tracking changes in memory and daily abilities [20], repeating the MoCA or MMSE in 6–12 months [21], and reassessing as the clinical picture clarifies [7].

You are allowed to seek a second opinion if uncertainty is blocking necessary planning. A good clinician will not be offended by this request.


## What you can do next

Before your next appointment, consider these questions. You will not get through all of them, and that is fine—prioritize what keeps you awake at night [7]:

- What specific dementia type do you think this is, and how sure are you? [22]
- Are we still looking for other causes like infections or vitamin deficiencies? [23]
- Should we treat depression, sleep apnea, or medication side effects? [24]
- How far along do we estimate the changes are? [25]
- What new symptoms like sudden confusion or falls should prompt an earlier call? [26]
- Are you suggesting any medications, and what should they help with—or not help with? [27]
- Would a research study for new Alzheimer's treatments be a fit? [28]
- When should we come back, and what improvements or changes should we expect? [29]

Write the answers down during the appointment. Memory is unreliable for everyone in high-stakes conversations, including you.
